# PURGATORY — Game Vision & Thematic Core

## Purpose

This document records the stable narrative and thematic principles that should guide worldbuilding, content design, quests, characters, locations, and major gameplay experiences in PURGATORY.

It is **not** a plot outline, lore encyclopedia, or implementation specification. Specific story facts may evolve; the principles below are the lens used to judge them.

---

## Core question

> **How do we live meaningful lives in a world that promises to preserve nothing?**

PURGATORY is interested in people living in a world where safety, places, relationships, institutions, achievements, and lives themselves can be lost.

The world does not guarantee permanence.

The game should not answer that fact with nihilism.

---

## Central thesis

> **Nothing we build is guaranteed to remain — but that is not a reason to stop building.**

Fragility does not make a thing meaningless.

A home can matter even if it is later destroyed. A community can matter even if it eventually disperses. A relationship can matter even if it ends. An attempt can matter even if it fails.

Meaning is not granted by permanence.

In PURGATORY, human beings continue to build, attach, cooperate, create, protect, love, work, hope, and begin again despite knowing — or having learned repeatedly — that none of those things are guaranteed to last.

---

## The thematic antagonist

The deepest antagonist is not destruction itself. Destruction, loss, change, and impermanence are part of the world's reality.

The antagonist is the belief that something temporary can be treated as though it were guaranteed to be permanent: the refusal to acknowledge fragility, the attempt to obtain absolute control over uncertainty, or the conviction that safety can finally be made unconditional.

This does **not** mean that people who build stable lives are foolish, or that accepting impermanence means becoming detached or passive.

The opposing human response is more difficult:

> **Know that something can break, and choose to care for it anyway.**

The game should repeatedly explore the tension between those positions rather than reduce it to a simple moral lesson.

---

## Human values

PURGATORY should make room for:

- resilience without pretending pain does not matter;
- hope without requiring naivety;
- rebuilding without erasing what was lost;
- community without guarantees of permanence;
- attachment despite the possibility of loss;
- courage that exists because outcomes are uncertain;
- practical survival alongside the desire for a life worth surviving for;
- people trying again after evidence that trying can fail.

The world may be harsh, but the game's view of people should not be contemptuous.

People continue because they want to live, not merely because the plot requires them to survive.

---

## Impermanence is not nihilism

A recurring failure mode to avoid is accidentally communicating:

> Everything eventually breaks, therefore nothing matters.

PURGATORY should instead create space for the opposite reflection:

> Things can matter precisely while they are finite, vulnerable, and dependent on human effort.

Destruction must therefore not automatically invalidate what came before it.

When a settlement falls, the question should not only be "why did they foolishly build it?" It may also be "what did its existence make possible while it lasted?"

The people who tried may have been wrong about permanence without being wrong to try.

---

## Narrative method

The theme should primarily be **experienced**, not explained.

Avoid turning major characters into mouthpieces who lecture the player about the philosophy of the game. Prefer:

- places whose histories embody the theme;
- people making different choices under uncertainty;
- institutions that help real people even while remaining fragile;
- small environmental details that reveal how people live;
- consequences that reframe earlier experiences;
- recurring motifs whose meaning becomes clearer over time;
- disagreement between characters who each possess part of the truth.

Lore should generally assume that the player character already belongs to this world. The user may learn facts gradually through context even when the character would consider those facts ordinary knowledge.

Do not force the character to ask artificial exposition questions merely because the user has not yet learned the answer.

---

## Character and faction lens

Major characters, communities, and factions can be tested against the core question:

**How does this person respond to a world that guarantees nothing?**

Useful responses may include:

- build again;
- cling harder;
- refuse attachment;
- accumulate control;
- preserve memory;
- exploit instability;
- protect others;
- retreat into denial;
- accept change;
- attempt to defeat change itself.

No single response should automatically map to hero or villain.

Characters become more interesting when their strategy is understandable and has both value and cost.

---

## Worldbuilding lens

The world should contain genuine civilization, not only ruins and survival camps.

People establish routines, settlements, trades, relationships, institutions, celebrations, ambitions, and ordinary lives. Some of these endure for long periods. Others fail.

This creates a world of **real but contingent stability** rather than a permanently apocalyptic landscape.

The player should be able to encounter both what people have lost and what they are currently succeeding at building.

A functioning place is not evidence that the world's fragility has disappeared. A destroyed place is not evidence that its builders achieved nothing.

---

## Design filter

For important narrative or content decisions, ask:

1. Does this idea reveal something about how people live under uncertainty?
2. Does it treat loss as consequential rather than decorative?
3. Does it preserve human agency rather than reducing characters to victims of the world?
4. Does it avoid equating impermanence with meaninglessness?
5. Can the player experience the idea through play, place, consequence, or character rather than only exposition?
6. Does it leave room for reflection instead of prescribing one explicit philosophical answer?

Not every quest or item needs to carry the central theme. The game needs humor, ordinary work, discovery, combat, social life, and simple pleasures as well. Those ordinary things are themselves part of what makes the larger theme meaningful.
